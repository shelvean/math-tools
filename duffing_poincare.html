Duffing Poincaré Section

One oscillator, one idea: a strobe light that flashes once every forcing cycle. Each flash drops a dot at the trajectory's current position. Watch those dots build up into a Poincaré section — the fingerprint that tells a periodic orbit apart from chaos.

The strobe-light idea

A driven oscillator never sits still, so its path through the phase plane \((x,\dot x)\) quickly becomes a tangle you can't read. The trick, due to Henri Poincaré, is to stop watching continuously and instead take snapshots at evenly spaced moments — like lighting a spinning wheel with a strobe.

Poincaré section (stroboscopic). The forcing \(\gamma\cos(\omega t)\) repeats every \(T=2\pi/\omega\). Record the state \((x,\dot x)\) once per forcing period, at \(t = 0,\,T,\,2T,\,3T,\dots\) Throw away everything in between. The collection of recorded points is the section.

This turns a continuous flow into a discrete map: each dot is sent to the next dot one period later. The messy loops disappear and the skeleton of the motion is laid bare.

How to read the picture

After the motion settles down (we discard an initial transient), the number of dots tells you everything:

  • One dot → the orbit repeats exactly every forcing period. This is a period-1 response: the strobe always catches the system in the same place.
  • Two dots → the orbit takes two forcing periods to repeat (period-2). The strobe alternates between two spots. \(k\) dots means period-\(k\).
  • A fuzzy cloud that never closes up → the orbit never repeats. The dots trace out a strange attractor: an intricate, self-similar set. This is deterministic chaos.

The Duffing oscillator

We use one concrete, physical system — a mass in a double-well spring, shaken periodically:

\(\ddot{x} + \delta\dot{x} + \alpha x + \beta x^3 = \gamma\cos(\omega t)\)

With \(\alpha=-1,\ \beta=1\) the restoring force \(-\alpha x-\beta x^3\) comes from a potential \(V(x)=\tfrac{\alpha}{2}x^2+\tfrac{\beta}{4}x^4\) with two wells (a bead that can rest in either of two valleys). The term \(\delta\dot x\) is friction; \(\gamma\cos(\omega t)\) is the periodic shaking. Only one knob matters for the story below: the drive strength \(\gamma\).

The route to chaos

Turn \(\gamma\) up slowly and the section changes in a famous, orderly way — the period-doubling cascade. A single dot splits into two, two into four, four into eight… the doublings pile up faster and faster (with these settings, \(\gamma\approx0.26\to0.27\to0.29\)) and, past a threshold near \(\gamma\approx0.30\), give way to the fractal dust of a strange attractor. But chaos is not the end of the story: scattered through the chaotic range are narrow periodic windows where order briefly returns — the \(\gamma=0.37\) preset lands in a period-5 window, and nudging the slider to \(\gamma\approx0.52\) finds a period-3 one — before chaos resumes. This same cascade appears in dripping faucets, heart arrhythmias, and electronic circuits — the Duffing oscillator is just the cleanest place to see it.

How to use this tool

  • Pick a preset, or drag the \(\gamma\) slider, to choose a drive strength.
  • Press ▶ Strobe to watch it live: the left panel shows the continuous trajectory with the strobe flashing once per cycle; the right panel keeps only the flashes — the section itself.
  • Press Fill in to skip the animation and plot thousands of section points at once.
  • The little clock shows the forcing phase; the section is sampled each time its hand returns to the top.

Numerics: classical 4th-order Runge–Kutta with a fixed step of \(T/400\) per forcing period, so every sample lands exactly one period apart. The first few dozen periods are discarded as transient.
