Van der Pol Oscillator

Simulate the second-order nonlinear equation \(\ddot{x} - \mu(1-x^2)\dot{x} + x = 0\) and observe the transition from sinusoidal oscillation to relaxation oscillations as \(\mu\) increases.

The Van der Pol Equation

The Van der Pol oscillator, introduced by Dutch physicist Balthasar van der Pol (1920), models self-sustained oscillations in electrical circuits with nonlinear damping. The second-order ODE is:

\[\ddot{x} - \mu(1-x^2)\dot{x} + x = 0, \qquad x(0)=x_0,\quad \dot{x}(0)=v_0.\]

Writing \(v = \dot{x}\), this is equivalent to the first-order system \(\dot{x}=v\), \(\dot{v}=\mu(1-x^2)v - x\).

Nonlinear Damping and the Limit Cycle

The term \(-\mu(1-x^2)\dot{x}\) acts as nonlinear damping:

  • When \(|x| < 1\): the factor \((1-x^2)>0\), so \(-\mu(1-x^2)\dot{x}\) is negative damping — energy is pumped into the system (shaded blue in the animation).
  • When \(|x| > 1\): the factor \((1-x^2)<0\), so damping is positive — energy is removed from the system (shaded red).

This self-regulating mechanism drives every trajectory (for \(\mu > 0\)) toward a unique stable limit cycle, regardless of initial conditions. The amplitude of the limit cycle approaches 2 as \(t\to\infty\).

Regimes

  • \(\mu = 0\): Simple harmonic oscillator — sinusoidal oscillations, no limit cycle, amplitude = \(|x_0|\).
  • \(\mu \ll 1\) (weakly nonlinear): Quasi-sinusoidal oscillations slowly converging to the limit cycle. Period \(\approx 2\pi\).
  • \(\mu \approx 1\) (Van der Pol): Clear nonlinear distortion, rapid approach to limit cycle.
  • \(\mu \gg 1\) (relaxation oscillations): The trajectory consists of slow drifts along the slow manifold \(v \approx \mu(x - x^3/3)\), interspersed with rapid jumps. Period \(T \approx (3-2\ln 2)\,\mu \approx 1.614\,\mu\).

Jacobian (Analytic)

The Jacobian of the system at \((x,v)\) is computed analytically:

\[J(x,v) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1-2\mu x v & \mu(1-x^2) \end{pmatrix}.\]

For large \(\mu\), the eigenvalue near \(x=0\) is approximately \(\mu\), making the system stiff (stiffness ratio \(\approx \mu\)).

Numerical Solvers

  • RK4 (classical Runge-Kutta, order 4) — used for \(\mu \le 5\). Four function evaluations per step, 4th-order accurate. Fixed step \(\Delta t = 0.01/(1+\mu/5)\). Stability requires \(\Delta t \lesssim 2.8/\mu\).
  • SDIRK2 (Singly Diagonally Implicit Runge-Kutta, order 2) — used for \(\mu > 5\). A-stable and L-stable: unconditionally stable for any step size. Two implicit stages with \(\gamma = 1-1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.2929\). Each stage solved via Newton iteration using the analytic Jacobian and exact \(2\times2\) linear solver. Adaptive step control via embedded error estimate.

The solver switches automatically at \(\mu = 5\). For \(\mu \ge 50\) (relaxation regime), SDIRK2 naturally takes large steps during slow phases and small steps during rapid transitions, making it far more efficient than any explicit method.

Time Scale Adaptation

The simulation end time adapts automatically: for small \(\mu\), \(T \approx 35\) shows several complete sinusoidal periods; for large \(\mu\), \(T \approx 4.5 \times 1.614\,\mu\) shows approximately 4–5 relaxation cycles. Playback speed similarly adapts so one period takes about 8 seconds on screen.
