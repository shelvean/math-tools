Calculus

Interactive visualizers for the ideas of calculus. Adjust each one live and watch a concept take shape, step by step. To begin, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, which links the area under a curve with the derivative.

Two matching visualizers for the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. Pick a function $f$ ($x$, $x^2$, $\sin x$, $\cos x$, the wavy Fresnel curve $\sin(\tfrac{\pi}{2}x^2)$, or one you type yourself), then move the limits of integration. Every value updates as you drag.

The two parts of the theorem

  • Part 1 - the derivative of an integral. Let $F(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ be the area collected from $a$ up to $x$. Then $F'(x)=f(x)$: the rate at which new area is added is exactly the height of the curve. Changing the starting point $a$ only slides $F$ up or down, so its slope - and therefore $f$ - stays the same.
  • Part 2 - evaluating a definite integral. If $F$ is any antiderivative of $f$ (that is, $F'(x)=f(x)$), then $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a)$. The total signed area from $a$ to $b$ equals the change in the antiderivative between those two points.

How the graphs are drawn

Each area and slope is computed to high accuracy, so you can watch the theorem actually hold on screen - even for the Fresnel curve, which has no antiderivative you can write with the usual functions.

Tool Descriptions

Tool 1 of 2

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1

$\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x)$

Slide $x$ to accumulate signed area under $f$ and watch the accumulation function $F(x)$ trace out with a tangent line of slope $f(x)$. Its numerically computed derivative rebuilds $f$ exactly - and moving the lower limit $a$ shifts $F$ up or down without ever changing the derivative.

Tool 2 of 2

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2

$\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a)$

The evaluation theorem. Slide $b$ to sweep out the signed area $\int_a^b f$ and see it equal the rise $F(b)-F(a)$ of an antiderivative. The area (by quadrature) and the antiderivative difference trace identical curves at the same scale.
